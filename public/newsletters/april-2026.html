Project Updates

The past few months have been busy as we prepare to launch AIRIS, our fast-slewing optical telescope on a high-altitude NASA balloon mission in Antarctica. Members are constantly machining parts and recently began testing our sensitive optics system in a cleanroom. AIRIS will piggyback on the ADAPT mission, a gamma-ray burst detector led by Physics Professor Jim Buckley. Gamma-ray bursts are the most energetic explosions in the universe, caused by black hole formation or neutron star collisions. While ADAPT detects gamma rays, AIRIS captures visible light from the early optical flash that appears seconds to minutes after the burst begins. This early signal provides key insight into the jet formed during the collapse, revealing its composition and how energy flows. Ground-based telescopes often miss these events due to communication delays, but AIRIS can begin imaging within about 30 seconds after detection by ADAPT, allowing for meaningful contributions to astrophysics research. Since AIRIS and ADAPT fly in December, we are currently working on our flight hardware and writing flight software. We will then test it over the summer at a NASA testing facility in Palestine, Texas. If all goes well, we will have some pretty amazing data to examine at the beginning of next year!

Kian Eghtesady suited up in a cleanroom holding the camera sensor for AIRIS.

Our partnership with our launch service provider, RIDE!, for SCALAR is official! Launch is under a year away, and the team is hard at work. SCALAR is a 1U CubeSat being built on the PROVES Kit, which serves as the foundation for a custom controls payload featuring three team-designed magnetorquers. By interacting with Earth’s magnetic field, these components provide a mass-efficient solution for developing semi-precise pointing and spinning algorithms. The SCALAR team is broken down into various subteams in order to streamline development and follow professional aerospace standards. The structures team just completed important vibration simulations, and the electrical team is finalizing two custom-wound coils, an embedded PCB coil, and their power electronics. The command and communications subteams are also busy writing flight software and developing operation modes. As the semester concludes, we are transitioning to rigorous testing to make sure our inaugural CubeSat makes it to space safely.

Andrew Press dimensioning and working on assembly for our prototype of SCALAR.

The team has been making progress on GS-2, a roof-mounted satellite communications system being developed to support future WashU Satellite missions. The project is interdisciplinary, bringing together mechanical, electrical, RF, and controls to ensure it accurately and reliably points at satellites and maintains communication links. The ground station will use a helical antenna about one meter long, forming a spiral structure that allows us to receive and send signals to our satellites. Critical Design Reviews underway and plans to begin assembly and initial testing by late April to early May. GS-2 is expected to be operational on the Lopata Hall roof early next semester.

Current Status

Our team is organized into different disciplines covering Systems, Software, Mechanical, Electrical, Mission Ops, and Physics. Within each discipline, members focus on a specific project. This ensures that each member’s expertise is applied where it is needed, while also enabling each member to contribute maximally to the team’s projects.

During the spring 2026 recruitment season, we welcomed 12 new members to the team, bringing our total to 62. These students were selected for their skillset and technical knowledge, and they have already begun contributing to our ongoing projects.
