Project Updates

Our first 1U CubeSat, SCALAR, has progressed greatly. Over the summer, members consistently submitted payload and integration deliverables to our launch partner, RIDE!. This semester, we are in full swing, dedicating our time to final assembly, software integration, and full system testing. Our electrical team is testing the flight boards, payload, radio, and power system. The structures team is preparing testing facilities and finalizing assembly procedures. Software teams are finalizing the flight software for sending data and controlling orbit modes, while ADCS develops 3-axis control algorithms utilizing our custom magnetorquers for on-orbit rotation. The team is gearing up for TVAC and vibration testing, and we are super excited for our 2027 launch. If you want to send your name to space onboard the CubeSat, get your boarding pass at washusatellite.com/space.

Warren Schindler and Beck Winkler working on assembly for SCALAR, our 1U CubeSat.

After successful rigorous summer testing at the NASA Columbia Scientific Balloon Facility, our near-space telescope, AIRIS, was shipped to Antarctica ahead of its winter launch. While it ships, WashU Satellite students are using a physical copy built from prototype parts of AIRIS to test firmware changes on the ground, minimizing the risk of uploading new algorithms in flight. Physics students have been working to generate accurate sample images, verify the burst detection pipeline, and model precise orientation. Software students are now implementing sun avoidance algorithms and finalizing the concept of operations pipeline, ensuring everything is perfectly ready for a successful mission this winter.

AIRIS mounted for testing at the NASA Columbia Scientific Balloon Facility in Palestine, Texas.

VECTOR is a future mission concept designed to validate the capacity for autonomous optical transient detection on-orbit. The mission concept originated as part of the University Nanosatellite Program’s Mission Concept course, which we sent former members Sophie Fendler, Jack Galloway, and Krystal Carranza to in 2024. The mission concept has now been handed off to the team in preparation for submission in Fall 2027.

Members at the VECTOR kickoff meeting, going over the mission’s constraints and design factors.

GS-2, a UHF helical antenna, is almost ready to be mounted on the roof of Crow Hall. This ground station will allow us to communicate with all of our future missions, including SCALAR and VECTOR. In order to communicate with satellites during the short time they pass over St. Louis, GS-2 must rotate to continuously point at them. The team had a chance to demonstrate this functionality during this year’s McKelvey in Action, where student groups like ours were able to showcase their projects to the local community.

Paul Fleck holding up the GS-2 helical antenna, soon to be mounted on Crow Hall.

Recent Events

In an exciting milestone for our VECTOR project, former members Sophie and Jack recently published a paper for the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Aerospace Conference. Their paper describes the VECTOR mission concept and introduces a novel approach to verifying transient detection in orbit. Sophie is now pursuing a PhD at Caltech while Jack is finishing his master’s at WashU before starting a full-time position as a spacecraft engineer.

On October 7th, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center research astrophysicist Dr. Carolyn Kierans spoke with our team. Dr. Kierans studies gamma-ray astronomy, working on NASA’s Compton Spectrometer and Imager (COSI) mission and leading the ComPair balloon instrument. We now have two projects focusing on advancing gamma-ray astronomy, so we are very grateful that she was able to share her relevant experience with our team.

Dr. Carolyn Kierans of NASA Goddard presenting to the team on gamma-ray astronomy.

WashU Satellite was featured in an article by the WashU Student Life newspaper, which talked about the club’s recent history and progress on our current projects. We are honored to have been featured in the newspaper, and you can hear directly from students who helped make SCALAR and AIRIS possible.

Josh Smith holding a WashU Satellite sign in front of GS-2 at the Club Fair.

With this many new members, we decided to host a competition in order to integrate everyone into the team and foster collaboration between subteams. Mechanical subteam members testing their paper airplanes shortly before the subteam competition.

On October 10th, we had our first social event of the year, where the different subteams competed in various events throughout the night, including a bottle flipping contest, a relay race, and a human knot untying race.

Conferences

From August 23rd to 26th, WashU Satellite sent 10 members spanning all our discipline subteams to the Small Satellite Conference in Salt Lake City, Utah. The team was able to network with aerospace professionals and potential sponsors. They also presented a poster, heard from other student groups, and bonded together outside of the conference. The members were Josh Smith, Oliver Yeaman, Matt Dunn, Peter Essa, Cooper Rodriguez, Aidan Moriarty, Allen Maldonado, Andrew Press, Aavik Wadivkar, and Warren Schindler.
